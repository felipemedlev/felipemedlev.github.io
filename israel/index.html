PART I

Understanding Israel

01

Israel at a glance

Israel is a small country on the eastern Mediterranean coast. It is a parliamentary democracy: voters elect a parliament, and the government needs its support. Israel is also the national home of the Jewish people. Many citizens are not Jewish.

Hebrew is the main state language. Arabic has a special legal status and is widely used by Arab citizens. Immigration has also made Russian, English, French and Amharic part of daily life. Jewish holidays shape the national calendar; Muslim, Christian and Druze communities have their own holidays.

Keep asking: How should a Jewish national identity and equal citizenship fit together?

02

The land: a useful mental map

Picture a long, narrow country. The Mediterranean coast is to the west. Tel Aviv is a major business and cultural center. Haifa is a northern port city. Jerusalem, Israel’s seat of government, lies in the hills. The Galilee is farther north; the Negev desert covers much of the south.

Egypt is southwest, Jordan east, and Lebanon and Syria north. Gaza is on the southwest coast. The West Bank lies east of the central coastal plain. “Judea and Samaria” is the name often used for the West Bank in Israeli official and right-wing speech.

Keep geography separate from sovereignty. East Jerusalem, the West Bank, Gaza and the Golan Heights have different legal and political situations. A line on a map may show military control rather than an agreed international border.

Keep asking: Does a map show an agreed border, a claim, or present control?

Water and the Golan

Israel relies on desalination, wastewater reuse and water management in a dry climate. The Golan was captured from Syria in 1967; Israel applied its law there in 1981. Most countries do not recognize Israeli sovereignty there. The United States recognized it in 2019.

PART II

Historical context

03

Before the state: why two national movements emerged

Jewish history and religion connect the Jewish people to this land. Ancient Jewish kingdoms existed here. After conquest and exile, large Jewish communities lived abroad—the diaspora—while Jewish communities also remained in the land. Many other peoples lived here across the centuries.

Modern Zionism grew in the late 1800s. It sought a Jewish national home, partly in response to antisemitism in Europe. The land was then under Ottoman rule and had a mainly Arab population. Palestinian Arab nationalism developed around people’s connection to the same land.

Britain’s 1917 Balfour Declaration supported a Jewish national home while referring to the rights of existing non-Jewish communities. Under the British Mandate, Jewish immigration and institution building expanded, alongside growing conflict. The Holocaust—the murder of six million Jews—made the need for refuge urgent. In 1947 the UN proposed partition into Jewish and Arab states. The main Jewish leadership accepted it; Arab leaders rejected it.

Keep asking: How can you recognize both peoples’ historical connections?

Three useful terms

Aliyah means Jewish immigration to Israel. The early immigration waves are called the First and Second Aliyah. Yishuv refers to the pre-state Jewish community. Its unions, schools and defense organizations helped build the later state.

Sources: UN · History of the Palestine question · US Holocaust Memorial Museum · Victims of the Holocaust

04

1948–1967: independence, displacement and state building

Israel declared independence on 14 May 1948. Fighting between Jewish and Arab communities had already begun; neighboring Arab armies then entered the war. For Israelis, this became the War of Independence. For Palestinians, mass displacement and loss of homes became the Nakba, meaning “catastrophe.” Many Palestinians fled or were expelled and could not return.

The new state absorbed Holocaust survivors and many Jews from Arab and Muslim countries. Their departures had different causes: persecution, expulsion in some places, insecurity and a wish to move to Israel. Many newcomers lived first in temporary camps, called ma’abarot.

David Ben-Gurion and the Mapai party led early state building. The army and public services developed under a strong state-led economy. Kibbutzim—collective communities—were influential. Arab citizens lived under military administration until 1966.

Two early controversies

The 1952 reparations agreement with West Germany divided Israelis over accepting compensation after the Holocaust. In the 1956 Suez war, Israel attacked Egypt alongside Britain and France, then withdrew from Sinai under international pressure.

Sources: UN · History of the Palestine question

05

1967–2000: territory, political change and peace efforts

In the 1967 Six-Day War, Israel captured the West Bank, East Jerusalem, Gaza, Sinai and the Golan. The occupation and the growth of settlements reshaped politics. In 1973, Egypt and Syria launched a surprise attack in the Yom Kippur War, shaking confidence in the leadership.

In 1977, Menachem Begin’s Likud defeated the long-dominant Labor camp. Peace with Egypt followed in 1979; Israel later returned Sinai. The 1982 Lebanon war and the First Intifada, beginning in 1987, brought new divisions. Large immigration waves from the former Soviet Union and Ethiopia changed society.

The 1993 Oslo process brought mutual recognition between Israel and the PLO and created limited Palestinian self-government. Israel and Jordan made peace in 1994. A Jewish extremist assassinated Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin in 1995. Attacks, settlement growth, mistrust and disputes over final borders weakened the peace process.

06

Since 2000: violence, withdrawal and division

The Second Intifada began in 2000 and included deadly attacks on Israeli civilians and major Israeli military operations. Israel built a security barrier. Its supporters stress fewer attacks; critics stress its route through the West Bank and restrictions on Palestinian life.

Israel removed its settlers and permanent military bases from Gaza in 2005. Hamas took control there in 2007. Repeated Gaza conflicts and the 2006 Lebanon war made security central to elections. Netanyahu returned as prime minister in 2009. The 2020 Abraham Accords opened formal relations with several Arab countries.

The 2023 judicial overhaul caused large protests. On 7 October 2023, Hamas-led attackers killed civilians and soldiers and took hostages. The Gaza war caused enormous Palestinian loss of life and destruction. By late September 2026, AP reported a fragile ceasefire dating from October 2025, with continued Israeli strikes and unresolved control of territory.

PART III

How the state works

07

The constitution: a system built in pieces

Israel has no single, complete written constitution. Instead, Basic Laws set out important institutions and some rights. The Declaration of Independence is a founding statement, but it is not a complete constitution. The Supreme Court reviews government actions and can review laws under constitutional rules.

Unlike some democracies, Israel has no second parliamentary chamber or federal states. A disciplined coalition can control both the cabinet and the Knesset. This makes the balance between elected power and legal limits especially important.

“Jewish and democratic” is a legal and political idea. People disagree about how national identity, religious authority, minority rights and equal citizenship should fit together.

Sources: IDI · Review of institutional changes, July 2026

08

The Knesset: 120 people who make the laws

The Knesset is Israel’s parliament. Its 120 members are called MKs. They vote on laws and the budget, question ministers, and work in committees. The coalition supports the government; the opposition challenges it and proposes alternatives.

Most bills pass through several readings and committee work. A government bill comes from the cabinet. A private member’s bill comes from an MK and usually needs an extra preliminary vote. Committees examine details that headlines often miss.

The Speaker manages parliamentary proceedings. Oversight means checking whether officials use power and money properly. A constructive no-confidence vote must support an alternative government; simple criticism does not remove a cabinet.

Keep asking: Does an MK’s voting record match their campaign promises?

Sources: IDI · How national elections work

09

The prime minister and government

The prime minister leads the cabinet. Ministers run policy areas such as finance, education and defense. The security cabinet handles major security questions. Civil servants carry out policy, often serving across several governments.

A ministry’s budget matters as much as a minister’s speech. The government proposes the state budget; the Knesset approves it. Coalition parties bargain over money, ministerial posts and policy commitments.

When a government loses support, a new government may be formed or an election may follow. Failure to pass a budget by the legal deadline can also trigger an election. The outgoing government normally continues until a replacement takes office.

10

The president and other public institutions

The president is mainly a ceremonial head of state, separate from the prime minister. After an election, the president consults parliamentary factions and gives an MK the task of trying to form a government.

The State Comptroller checks public administration. The Central Bureau of Statistics produces official data. The Competition Authority deals with competition rules. The Israel Land Authority manages much public land. Regulators supervise areas such as finance and communications.

The Bank of Israel sets monetary policy and supervises banks. Its central goal is price stability. It does not set grocery prices directly. Public broadcasting is another important institution: the debate is how to fund it while protecting editorial independence.

11

Courts, judges and legal advisers

Magistrates’ courts hear many everyday cases. District courts hear more serious cases and appeals. The Supreme Court hears appeals and also sits as the High Court of Justice—Bagatz—to hear challenges to public authorities.

The attorney general advises the government and has responsibilities at the top of the public prosecution system. The State Attorney’s Office handles public prosecution and legal representation. Ministry legal advisers help officials act within the law.

The argument over judicial change concerns appointments, review of government actions and the independence of legal advice. Some reforms became laws; others remained proposals or faced court challenges. A proposal, a passed law and a law currently in force are three different things.

12

Local government: politics close to home

Municipalities run cities; local councils serve smaller towns; regional councils serve groups of communities. Mayors and councils are elected separately from national elections. Their work includes local services, parts of education and welfare, and planning.

Arnona is a local property tax. Its rules and discounts are important to household budgets. Wealthier authorities often have stronger business tax income. Poorer authorities may depend more on central government transfers.

Arab towns, Haredi towns and rural communities face different needs. Planning restrictions, transport, school capacity and safety can matter more locally than national slogans. Settlement regional councils also operate within the wider territorial dispute.

13

How your vote becomes a seat

You vote for one candidate list, not directly for a prime minister. The whole country is one electoral district. Lists may contain one party or an alliance. Their candidates are ranked before election day; voters cannot change that order on the ballot.

There are 120 seats. A list needs at least 3.25% of valid votes to enter the Knesset. A list below this threshold receives no seats. Seats are then divided among qualifying lists in proportion to their votes. Israel’s Bader–Ofer method distributes the remaining seats through a highest-average calculation; it can affect the last seat or two, so a simple percentage multiplied by 120 is only an approximation.

Parties sometimes sign surplus-vote agreements. These affect the distribution of remaining seats if both lists qualify. They do not rescue a list below the threshold. A “wasted vote” usually means a vote for a list that fails to enter parliament; it is still a valid political expression.

Keep asking: Is the party you support safely above the threshold?

Primaries and the next step

In a primary, party members help choose candidates. Other parties use a leader, committee or religious council. After the official results, the president consults the factions and selects an MK to try to form a government. That MK initially has 28 days, with a possible extension of up to 14 days. The largest list does not automatically get the premiership.

14

Coalitions: why 61 is the famous number

The Knesset has 120 seats. Each person in a seat is an MK, a Member of the Knesset. MKs vote on laws, on the state budget, and on whether a government may take office. Chapter 8 describes their other work.

Half of 120 is 60. 61 is one seat past half. When 61 MKs vote together, they beat the other 59, even if every MK is in the room. With 60 on each side, the vote is a tie, and a tie fails. That is why headlines treat 61 as the number that makes a government safe.

No party has won 61 seats by itself in a national election. Parties build a coalition: several parties that agree to govern as one government. A group of parties planning to sit together is also called a bloc. The partners divide the ministries, agree on spending, and sign a coalition agreement. That paper is a promise among the parties. It applies to the whole country once the Knesset turns the promises into laws or into the budget.

A small party gains power when the others need its seats to reach 61. A handful of seats can decide whether a government exists. The seats are the first test. The partners must also accept one another’s conditions. In Israeli debate these conditions are called red lines. Common red lines are Benjamin Netanyahu as prime minister, policy toward the Palestinians, the public role of religion, who must serve in the army, and support from Arab parties.

How a government takes office

After the election, the president gives one MK the task of forming a government. That MK presents a prime minister, ministers, and policy guidelines to the Knesset. The Knesset then holds a confidence vote. The government takes office when more MKs vote yes than no. Absent MKs and abstaining MKs count for neither side.

A bloc with 61 seats can win even when the other 59 all vote no. A smaller bloc can also win, when enough opponents are absent or abstain. Example: 50 vote yes, 49 vote no, and 21 do not vote. The government is approved, because 50 is larger than 49.

If the parties inside the cabinet hold fewer than 61 seats, Israel has a minority government. It continues while MKs outside the cabinet vote with it, or abstain, on the votes that matter. On an ordinary day, the side with more MKs in the room wins. If the full opposition attends, a cabinet with 60 seats or fewer can lose.

Some parties remain outside the cabinet and still promise to protect the government and to pass its budget. The usual name for this is confidence and supply. Confidence means they will vote for the government’s survival. Supply means they will vote for its budget. They can do both without taking ministries.

How a government is replaced

Speeches against the government leave it in office. Replacement uses a constructive vote of no confidence. The motion must name a full alternative: a new prime minister and a new set of ministers. At least 61 MKs must vote for that alternative. The motion still needs those 61 yes votes when some chairs are empty. If it passes, the new government takes office at once.

The two votes use different counts. Forming a government needs more yes votes than no votes. Replacing a government needs 61 MKs lined up behind one alternative. Opponents can dislike the prime minister and still leave the cabinet in place, until they agree on who should replace it.

After the confidence vote, the budget is the practical test. If the Knesset misses the legal deadline for the state budget, the Knesset is dissolved and Israel holds an election. A working coalition is a group that can pass that budget and remain together.

15

Left, right and center: the divides that matter

Israeli politics has several dividing lines. A party can be right-wing on security, liberal on religion and willing to fund generous welfare. “Anti-Netanyahu” describes a leadership preference, not one ideology.

Territory, security and Palestinians
The right generally favors lasting Israeli control and settlement expansion; the Zionist left favors territorial compromise and a negotiated Palestinian state. The center emphasizes security conditions, regional arrangements and pragmatic steps. Opposition to Hamas alone does not tell you a party’s long-term Gaza or West Bank plan.
Religion and state
Haredi parties defend Orthodox institutions and Torah-study autonomy. Secular parties seek civil marriage, transport choices and changes to religious authority. Religious Zionists combine Jewish public identity with territorial nationalism and generally value army service; they are a different political family from Haredim.
Military service and public funding
Who must serve, what counts as civilian service, and whether schools must teach core subjects to receive funding divide potential partners. These disputes concern identity, workforce participation and the burden on reservists, not simply one draft bill.
Courts and democratic institutions
Judicial-change advocates say elected governments need greater freedom to govern. Opponents argue that Israel’s single chamber, coalition discipline and incomplete constitution make independent courts and legal advisers essential. Ask which specific power is being transferred and what check replaces it.
Economy and social protection
Market competition, taxation, housing, benefits and public services form another scale. Haredi parties are socially conservative but favor community spending; a secular security hawk may favor market reforms. Coalition budgets can depart from party branding.
Leadership, accountability and Arab participation
Views on Netanyahu, responsibility for 7 October and whether Arab parties may help sustain a government cut across older labels. Arab citizens are a diverse electorate; Ra’am, Hadash, Ta’al and Balad are not interchangeable.

Family origin, religion, location and income help explain voting patterns but do not determine an individual’s vote. Compare the parties’ actual coalition choices in the profiles, then examine the specific policy debates in Part X.

Five differences that make the map easier to read

  • Likud versus Yisrael Beiteinu: both oppose a Palestinian state and want more settlements. Lieberman adds a map: sovereignty in Area C, and Jordan running daily life in Areas A and B. The governing divide is still Haredi power, civil freedoms and Netanyahu.
  • Shas and UTJ versus Religious Zionism: all defend Orthodox marriage and conversion. Shas will now accept a draft for yeshiva dropouts. UTJ opposes drafting them. Religious Zionism puts settlement and army service much closer to the center, and wants those who serve to come first for benefits.
  • Beyahad versus Yashar! versus the Democrats: all seek alternative leadership, and they do not share one West Bank plan. Beyahad treats Area C as future Israeli land and rejects a Palestinian state. Yashar would grow the main settlement blocs but would not take charge of millions of Palestinian civilians. The Democrats oppose annexation and want separation.
  • Ra’am versus the Joint List: both seek Arab equality and Palestinian statehood. Ra’am combines religious conservatism with a record of formal governing-coalition participation; the Joint List combines distinct secular-left and national-democratic traditions with different cooperation strategies.
  • Smotrich versus Ben-Gvir: both want full sovereignty and no Palestinian state. Religious Zionism centers on more settlers and on making unauthorized outposts legal. Otzma also wants Palestinians to leave, and puts policing at the center of its appeal. They are separate 2026 lists.

Watch the gap between a preference and a condition. A party may favor civil marriage, universal service or independent courts but accept a coalition agreement that postpones reform. The useful question is what it would insist on, what it would trade, and whether its past decisions support that claim.

LikudBenjamin NetanyahuNational right
Ideology and voter base
A nationalist party with a market-oriented tradition and a broad Jewish electorate, including many traditional and Mizrahi voters. Support reflects security views, attachment to Netanyahu and resentment of older political elites; none describes every voter.
Security and Palestinian policy
In the 2026 campaign Netanyahu frames the choice as a Jewish state or a Palestinian state, and he opposes Palestinian statehood under current conditions. Settlement building has continued under governments led by Likud. In September 2026 the party refused to answer a questionnaire on annexation, outposts, Palestinian building in Area C and a final map, so it has not published one binding plan. Its record also includes peace with Egypt and the Gaza withdrawal. A nationalist label does not explain every governing decision.
Religion, civil freedoms and service
It has not published a 2026 position on civil marriage or conversion. Coalition deals keep Orthodox control of marriage, and the government restored the Chief Rabbinate’s exclusive kosher certification in July 2026. Knesset Speaker Amir Ohana, of Likud, voted for a civil marriage bill in December 2025. On Shabbat the party told ynet it wants Israel connected on the six working days, while keeping Shabbat as it has been since the founding of the state. On the draft it declined the September 2026 questionnaire. In July 2025 Communications Minister Shlomo Karhi described a coalition law to draft about 17,000 Haredi men within three years. That is a target, not service for everyone.
Institutions and public spending
It declined the September 2026 court questionnaire. Justice Minister Yariv Levin’s 2025 law changed the committee that picks judges and lets appointments go ahead without the sitting justices’ consent. He has said he can wait until about two thirds of Supreme Court seats fall vacant. No written 2026 economic platform was found. The party charter supports a competitive market and limited government involvement. Governing budgets also include religious, settlement and other sectoral funds.
History, governing choices and controversies

The 1973 alliance brought together Begin’s Herut and other parties. Its 1977 victory ended Labor’s dominance.

Begin made peace with Egypt; Sharon’s Gaza withdrawal divided Likud, and he founded Kadima in 2005. Netanyahu governed in 1996–1999 and 2009–2021, returning in late 2022. His ongoing corruption trial, which he contests, and responsibility for the failures preceding 7 October are central disputes.

Main difference and coalition role
Unlike the secular right wing lists, Likud builds its majority with Shas, United Torah Judaism, Religious Zionism and Otzma Yehudit. Those deals are why marriage and the draft stay open to compromise. The test for voters is whether security credentials and continuity outweigh demands for accountability and a change in those deals.

Together / BeyahadNaftali Bennett and Yair LapidRight–center alliance
Ideology and voter base
The joint 2026 list combines Bennett’s right-wing tradition with Lapid’s secular center. Seeks liberal right-wing voters, the middle class and voters prioritizing administrative competence and a change of leadership.
Security and Palestinian policy
The joint list says it supports Jewish settlement wherever it is legal, opposes a Palestinian state and opposes handing land to the Palestinians. It also opposes removing Jewish communities built legally. On sovereignty it points to Bennett’s 2012 plan: Area C would become part of Israel, but leaders would choose the moment. Palestinian building without permits in Area C would be removed. Israeli outposts on land the state treats as lawful would be approved. Building on private land without permission would be taken down. Lapid backed two states in 2022 and told AP in 2026 that this was not feasible in the coming years. The joint answer above is the list’s current line.
Religion, civil freedoms and service
Lapid says Israel should have a civil marriage option beside religious marriage, and that equal rights for every couple should be legislated in the first 100 days. Before the merger, Bennett said cities should choose whether they want public transport on Shabbat. On service the joint list says every citizen, Arab or Haredi, will enlist in the army or do civilian national service, with no exemptions.
Institutions and public spending
It wants the powers of the Knesset, the government and the Supreme Court written down, a Basic Law on how legislation works and when the court may intervene, and repeal of the 2025 appointments law, which it says would politicize the courts. It would make the Declaration of Independence a Basic Law. On prices it promises to cut food prices by 30 percent within three years and to break up food cartels. Treat the figure as a campaign pledge: ask what law, what budget and what timetable would deliver it.
History, governing choices and controversies

Bennett led Jewish Home, then split with Ayelet Shaked to create New Right, which missed the April 2019 threshold. He returned through Yamina.

Lapid founded Yesh Atid in 2012 and was finance minister in Netanyahu’s 2013 government. Both helped form the 2021 government with right, center, left and Ra’am; defections ended its majority in 2022, and Lapid succeeded Bennett before the election. Critics on the right condemned Bennett’s coalition choice; supporters saw a necessary compromise.

Main difference and coalition role
A joint ballot is closer cooperation than the 2021 rotation. It does not erase the gap between Bennett and Lapid. Both have ruled out sitting with the Haredi parties. They governed with Ra’am in 2021. That record is not a promise to do it again. Bennett and Eisenkot still compete over who would lead an alternative government.

Yashar!Gadi EisenkotSecurity-minded center
Ideology and voter base
A new party led by a former IDF chief of staff. Its appeal centers on security experience, public service and institutional stability rather than an established independent governing record. Its prospective electorate crosses older party loyalties.
Security and Palestinian policy
The party says it will strengthen the large settlement blocs and found new communities when that is legal and matches security advice. A Palestinian state is not on its agenda, and it has no plan to remove communities in a diplomatic deal. It opposes steps that would make Israel a binational state, and says Israel should not take charge of daily life for about 2 million Palestinians. Unauthorized Israeli outposts would be reviewed one by one. Palestinian building without permits in Area C would face enforcement.
Religion, civil freedoms and service
The party supports civil marriage, divorce and marriage for same sex couples, and would leave Shabbat decisions to each city. Eisenkot says he is not religious, comes from a traditional family and respects Jewish tradition. On service it wants national service for everyone. The only deferral would be up to 3 percent of each annual cohort for outstanding individuals, about 2,200 people in a national cohort of about 70,000. State incentives would be revoked for those who evade.
Institutions and public spending
It would repeal the 2025 judge selection law and replace it through a Basic Law. It opposes an override clause. Judicial review would be set out in a Basic Law on the judiciary, next to a Basic Law on legislation. On the economy it stresses the periphery and narrower social gaps, with equal opportunities, equal rights and equal duties. That is a direction, not a complete tax plan.
History, governing choices and controversies

Eisenkot entered parliament through National Unity in 2022. He joined the emergency government after 7 October and left with Gantz in June 2024 amid disputes over wartime strategy.

He resigned from National Unity in June 2025, creating a separate political path. His campaign must answer both how his leadership differs from Gantz’s and what he achieved while inside wartime decision-making.

Main difference and coalition role
Eisenkot’s conditions for partners include recognizing Hamas as a terror organization that must be dismantled, accepting the Declaration of Independence, and accepting Israel as a Jewish and democratic state. He has said he does not currently see an Arab or Haredi party, including Ra’am, that meets those conditions, so Abbas would not be in a government he leads. Personal standing in a poll is separate from the seats those conditions leave available.

Yisrael BeiteinuAvigdor LiebermanSecular right
Ideology and voter base
Founded in 1999 with strong support from former Soviet immigrants. Now also seeks a wider secular electorate. Combines security hawkishness, private enterprise and opposition to Orthodox control of personal life.
Security and Palestinian policy
The party wants more settlement in the West Bank, including construction in E1, between Jerusalem and Ma’ale Adumim. New communities would depend on security and planning, not a promise to build everywhere. It supports Israeli sovereignty in Area C, stronger action against Palestinian building without permits, and removal of Israeli building that lacks authorization. It opposes a Palestinian state. On 5 October 2026 Lieberman set out the diplomatic plan: cancel the Oslo Accords, dismantle the Palestinian Authority, keep Israeli security control and territorial continuity across the West Bank, and ask Jordan to run public order and daily life in Areas A and B. In Gaza he wants Hamas destroyed, including its civilian structures, and then an outside body acceptable to Arab states and other countries to run the Strip. In one interview he said Jordan should run Areas B and C. He later said that was a slip and restated Areas A and B. Older ideas about moving populations are not this 2026 plan, but they still shape how Arab citizens hear the party.
Religion, civil freedoms and service
It demands civil marriage and divorce inside Israel. On Shabbat, Tel Aviv would not impose buses on Bnei Brak, and Bnei Brak would not impose its way of life on Tel Aviv. It also backs the Western Wall compromise drafted by Avichai Mandelblit and Natan Sharansky. On service, every person at age 18, Jewish, Muslim, Christian, Druze or Circassian, would do military or civilian service. No exemptions, no quotas and no deals.
Institutions and public spending
On the courts it wants a written constitution approved in a national referendum. Amendments would need at least 80 of the 120 Knesset members. On the economy, published descriptions give it a right wing fiscal line and support for private enterprise. That is not a pledge of large new welfare spending.
History, governing choices and controversies

A frequent former Netanyahu partner, it ran jointly with Likud in 2013. Lieberman held foreign and defense portfolios.

In 2019 his dispute with Haredi parties over conscription helped block a Netanyahu coalition. In 2021 he joined Bennett–Lapid and served as finance minister, illustrating how religion-and-state disputes can override a shared right-wing security outlook.

Main difference and coalition role
Its opposition to Netanyahu is not a move toward the left on security or on settlements. The draft line, no exemptions and no deals, is what blocks a return to governing with the Haredi parties. Disagreement inside the opposition over Arab party support can still decide whether those leaders can reach 61.

The DemocratsYair GolanZionist left
Ideology and voter base
A liberal and social-democratic list formed through Labor–Meretz cooperation in 2024. Appeals especially to secular progressive voters, peace-camp voters and supporters of stronger public services. Labor and Meretz retain organizational identities.
Security and Palestinian policy
The party says years of managing the conflict failed, and that Netanyahu strengthened Hamas while weakening the Palestinian Authority. It wants the army and the Shin Bet free to act against armed groups, together with a diplomatic strategy. It would remove illegal outposts that create friction. Its platform says it will stop state funding for new outposts and farms it calls illegal, and return West Bank civilian control from Smotrich’s settlement administration to the army. The stated aim is separation, inside a regional move with the United States and moderate Arab states, with security guarantees. Golan treats a Palestinian state as a later step. Lawmaker Gilad Kariv has been more direct: divide the land and establish a demilitarized Palestinian state as part of a regional agreement. That is clearer than the other large opposition lists, and less immediate than the Arab parties’ call for statehood now.
Religion, civil freedoms and service
It would promote civil marriage, run Shabbat buses in a limited form in secular towns, and extend recognition of conversion beyond the Chief Rabbinate. The duty to serve the state, it says, should apply to every citizen inside one national and equal framework. These civil and service positions are separate from its West Bank line.
Institutions and public spending
On the courts it says the urgent problem is delay, overloaded dockets and too few judges, not excessive judicial power. It wants an intermediate court of appeals and a full definition of the separation of powers. On the economy it wants a lower cost of living, better social services and a rebuilt public sector, plus equal citizenship and changes to the Nation-State Law.
History, governing choices and controversies

Labor descends from Mapai, which dominated early governments, and later led Rabin’s Oslo process. Rabin’s 1995 assassination, the failed 2000 peace summit and Second Intifada shaped the left’s decline and security debates.

Meretz was established in 1992 and governed with Rabin. Both joined Bennett–Lapid in 2021; Meretz failed the 2022 threshold. Their new alliance addresses fragmentation as well as ideological renewal.

Main difference and coalition role
Golan says the Democrats will not sit with the parties of the current government, and that the Haredi parties have disqualified themselves. He is open to Ra’am, which sets the party apart from Eisenkot. An earlier remark, that he would sit with Haredi parties if Likud, Smotrich and Ben-Gvir were out, is the line he then pulled back. In talks with right wing partners, annexation, spending and Arab participation are the bargains.

ShasAryeh Deri; Council of Torah SagesSephardi Haredi
Ideology and voter base
Founded in 1984 to challenge Sephardi underrepresentation in Ashkenazi Haredi institutions. Combines Haredi school networks with a broader traditional Mizrahi constituency and an emphasis on social assistance.
Security and Palestinian policy
Shas says it belongs to the right wing bloc, opposes a Palestinian state and has supported settlement steps advanced by Smotrich and others. It does not campaign mainly on the West Bank. In coalition talks, yeshivas, religious authority and welfare usually come first. Its years in governments led by Labor show that today’s right wing label is not the whole diplomatic history.
Religion, civil freedoms and service
It opposes civil marriage and wants conversion kept inside the Orthodox establishment. It opposes wider non-Orthodox prayer at the Western Wall. It championed the July 2026 law that returned kosher certification to the Chief Rabbinate. On the draft it now says students who have left yeshiva can no longer be kept from service. Full time Torah students are still the group it protects. That is a real difference from United Torah Judaism, which opposes drafting dropouts as well.
Institutions and public spending
Deri says the courts should be reformed so power returns to the people and their elected representatives. There is no detailed new judicial platform. On spending, Deri has said a poor Haredi child should receive at least the food support a poor Arab child receives. Social conservatism here comes with large public budgets for families, schools and community services.
History, governing choices and controversies

Rabbi Ovadia Yosef shaped its religious authority and pragmatic coalition tradition. It served in governments led by both Labor and Likud, including Rabin’s before leaving in 1993.

It reached 17 seats in 1999. Deri’s bribery conviction and later tax-offense conviction are major controversies; in 2023 the Supreme Court ruled against his ministerial appointment and Netanyahu removed him.

Main difference and coalition role
It shares the Orthodox marriage line with United Torah Judaism, but it will now accept a draft for yeshiva dropouts and it speaks for a wider traditional Mizrahi public. It is with Netanyahu in this election. It has also sat in governments led by Labor, so today’s bloc is not its whole history.

Ideology and voter base
An alliance of Hasidic Agudat Israel and mainly Lithuanian non-Hasidic Degel HaTorah. Rabbinic authorities and factional negotiations matter alongside the politician heading the ballot.
Security and Palestinian policy
The alliance has no single West Bank platform. Agudat Yisrael, the Hasidic faction, generally leans further right. Degel HaTorah, the Lithuanian faction, is more moderate. Neither treats settlement policy as the center of the campaign. Both focus on Haredi concerns, above all yeshiva study and exemptions from the draft. Joining Sharon’s government during the Gaza withdrawal shows that Haredi autonomy is not the same thing as settlement nationalism.
Religion, civil freedoms and service
It opposes civil marriage, keeps conversion Orthodox, and opposes wider non-Orthodox prayer at the Western Wall. Moshe Gafni, sponsor of the Torah study Basic Law, called that law the state’s moral compass. Yitzhak Goldknopf has said no sanction will stop the voice of the Torah.
Institutions and public spending
It says the Supreme Court has accumulated too much power and that this authority should be reconsidered and defined more clearly. Spending priorities are Haredi education and welfare: yeshivas, independent schools, housing and family support. Rabbis and the two factions set what is negotiable.
History, governing choices and controversies

The two factions have run together since 1992 after their earlier organizational split. UTJ left Barak’s coalition in 1999 over transport of a turbine on Shabbat.

It also participated in the Sharon government that implemented Gaza disengagement, showing why Haredi politics should not be equated with settlement nationalism. Religious education and conscription remain recurring sources of coalition pressure.

Main difference and coalition role
Its electorate is narrower and more tightly organized than Shas’s. Both factions left the government in July 2025 because the draft law was stalled. Alignment with Netanyahu depends on rabbinic approval and on a draft arrangement the factions can accept. Torah study autonomy, not settlement nationalism, is the center of the party.

Ideology and voter base
Links settlement nationalism and religious identity; Zehut brings Feiglin’s distinctive libertarian economic tradition. Appeals to parts of the national-religious public and hard-right voters, who also vote for other lists.
Security and Palestinian policy
The party rejects a Palestinian state and wants full Israeli sovereignty over the West Bank, plus hundreds of thousands more settlers. It says that in this Knesset it approved what it calls all young settlements, declared 104 new communities, set up 160 farms and approved 3,401 homes in E1 to connect Jerusalem and Ma’ale Adumim. Territory, settlements and religious national identity are the program, more than they are for Likud. The 2026 ballot is separate from Otzma, so each list must clear the threshold alone. A vote split on the right can waste seats even when the two lists want a similar government.

Otzma YehuditItamar Ben-GvirFar right
Ideology and voter base
A Jewish nationalist party emphasizing policing, sovereignty and forceful security measures. Appeals to hard-right voters, including some younger voters frustrated with established parties; it does not represent all religious voters.
Security and Palestinian policy
The party wants new communities in every part of the West Bank and opposes removing communities or farms. It wants full Israeli sovereignty, the Palestinian Authority stripped of assets and weapons, and state action to encourage what it calls voluntary migration from the West Bank and Gaza. That means it wants Palestinians to leave. Unauthorized Israeli outposts would be made legal. Judge those powers against equal treatment of Arab citizens and limits on police discretion.
Religion, civil freedoms and service
In 2022 Ben-Gvir said the state is Jewish and marriages should follow religious law. The published platform says nothing on marriage, conversion or Shabbat. MK Limor Son Har-Melech sponsored the July 2026 law allowing graduate programs that separate men and women. On the draft, the party points to police and Border Police tracks that keep Shabbat, kosher food, prayer, Torah study and gender separation, including an ultra Orthodox company. That is not a full draft law.
Institutions and public spending
It told ynet it was still preparing a detailed judicial platform. Ben-Gvir supported the 2023 overhaul, opposed softening it, and called the July 2023 reasonableness law just the beginning. When the 2025 judge selection law passed, he said the government must continue the rest of the overhaul. A detailed 2026 budget is not out. The platform talks about cutting regulation and bureaucracy, and about spending defense savings on infrastructure, industry and small business.
History, governing choices and controversies

Grew from a far-right activist milieu and repeatedly missed the threshold before Ben-Gvir entered parliament on the 2021 Religious Zionism joint list. The alliance expanded in 2022, bringing him the national-security ministry.

His convictions for racist incitement and support for a terrorist organization are central to criticism of his legitimacy. He resigned over the January 2025 Gaza ceasefire, demonstrating a willingness to pressure Netanyahu from the right.

Main difference and coalition role
It shares the sovereignty goal with Smotrich and adds policing, gender separation in public institutions, and pressure on Netanyahu from the right. The January 2025 resignation over the Gaza ceasefire shows that pressure. A threat to leave does not by itself produce an election.

Political turning points: why today’s alliances look this way

Today’s competition combines older social divisions with a prolonged argument over leadership and democratic institutions. Use these turning points as a framework; the individual party histories are in Chapter 16.

  1. 1948–1977Mapai and its Labor successors dominated state building. Their institutions helped absorb immigrants but also generated resentment over unequal treatment and elite control. Likud’s 1977 victory transformed representation and broke that dominance.
  2. 1990s–2000sOslo, Rabin’s assassination, the Second Intifada and Gaza disengagement reshaped confidence in territorial compromise. Immigration from the former Soviet Union strengthened secular immigrant politics. Splits and new centrist lists repeatedly reorganized the party map.
  3. 2019–2021Repeated elections exposed incompatible coalition commitments. Disputes over Netanyahu and Haredi conscription prevented otherwise ideologically similar parties from governing together. Gantz’s 2020 unity arrangement failed before the promised rotation.
  4. 2021–2022Bennett–Lapid united right, center, left and Ra’am around replacing Netanyahu. Its narrow majority and internal disagreements made individual defections decisive. Netanyahu returned after the November 2022 election.
  5. 2022 resultLikud won 32 seats, Shas 11, UTJ 7 and the combined Religious Zionism–Otzma–Noam list 14: a 64-seat bloc. Meretz and Balad missed the threshold. These are election results, not today’s polls; the far-right list now contests in separate frameworks.
  6. 2023–2026The judicial crisis and 7 October made institutional trust and security accountability central. Gantz and Eisenkot joined, then left, the wartime emergency government. New opposition alliances now compete on competence, service, leadership and the terms of replacing Netanyahu.

How to read today’s arguments: a party can reverse a previous coalition choice without changing every ideological belief. Ask whether a proposed partnership is motivated by policy, leadership, an emergency or access to budgets—and which promise its past decisions make harder to trust.

PART V

Security and the conflict

19

Who handles security?

The government sets national policy. The defense minister oversees the defense establishment. The IDF—the Israel Defense Forces—is led by the chief of the General Staff. Military intelligence provides military assessments.

Mossad focuses on intelligence and operations abroad. Shin Bet deals mainly with internal security and counterterrorism, including in the occupied territories. The police handle crime and public order. Border Police is a police force with security duties.

Home Front Command gives civilian protection instructions. The National Security Council advises the prime minister and coordinates policy. Military expertise does not remove the government’s duty to make and explain decisions.

Keep asking: Who made the decision, who carried it out, and who checks it?

20

Military and national service

Many Jewish men and women are conscripted, with exemptions and different arrangements. Druze and Circassian men are generally subject to conscription. Most Arab citizens are not routinely called up; some volunteer. National civilian service is another route for eligible people.

Reservists return to duty after their regular service. Long periods away affect families, study and employment. Hesder combines religious study with military service. A lone soldier has limited close-family support in Israel.

Service length and new-immigrant obligations depend on current rules and personal circumstances. For politics, the central question is how to share the military, economic and family burden fairly. Haredi conscription is explained in chapter 47.

Keep asking: Does “service for all” specify who serves, in what role, and with what enforcement?

21

The conflict: the basic questions

Israeli citizenship and Palestinian identity are different concepts. Some Israeli citizens identify as Palestinian. Most Palestinians in the West Bank and Gaza are not Israeli citizens and do not vote in Knesset elections.

The PLO represents the Palestinian national movement internationally. Fatah is a major party within it. The Palestinian Authority, created through Oslo, administers parts of the West Bank. Hamas is a separate Islamist political and armed movement. It is designated a terrorist organization by Israel, the United States and the European Union.

A two-state solution means Israel and a Palestinian state alongside it. One-state proposals differ radically: equal citizenship for everyone, a federation, or permanent control without equal rights are not the same proposal.

Hard questions include borders, Jerusalem, settlements, refugees and security. Israeli arguments emphasize protection from attacks and Jewish self-determination. Palestinian arguments emphasize freedom, equal rights and national self-determination. Understanding both does not require accepting attacks on civilians.

Keep asking: What rights and security would people have under this proposal?

Sources: UN · History of the Palestine question

22

The West Bank: one territory, different authorities

Under Oslo, Area A was assigned Palestinian civil and internal security administration; Area B Palestinian civil administration and shared security responsibilities; Area C Israeli civil and security control. These labels do not mean Palestinian sovereignty. Israeli military operations also take place in Areas A and B.

The Palestinian Authority runs services in parts of the territory. Israeli military authorities and the Civil Administration exercise extensive powers. Israeli settlers generally come under Israeli civil law for many purposes; Palestinians face a different system, including military law.

Settlements are Israeli civilian communities beyond the 1949 armistice line. Outposts may lack authorization under Israeli rules. Most of the international community considers the settlements illegal. Israel disputes key legal conclusions. In 2024, the ICJ’s advisory opinion found Israel’s continued presence in the occupied Palestinian territory unlawful. An advisory opinion is not a domestic criminal judgment.

Keep asking: Does a proposed annexation say what citizenship and rights Palestinians would receive?

Life under the system

Checkpoints, permits and road access affect work, schools and medical care. Israel cites security needs; Palestinians describe restrictions on freedom and daily life. Settlement supporters cite history, religious attachment and security. Critics point to unequal rights, land disputes and the weakening of a possible Palestinian state.

Sources: ICJ · Advisory opinion, 19 July 2024

23

Gaza and regional security

Withdrawal in 2005 did not settle Gaza’s status. Hamas took power in 2007 after conflict with Fatah. Israel and Egypt imposed access restrictions; Israel cites the threat from Hamas. Restrictions, repeated wars and destruction have severely affected civilian life.

In 2026, debates include Hamas’s disarmament, Israeli withdrawal, who governs Gaza, civilian protection and rebuilding. AP’s August reporting described a stalled ceasefire process and Israel’s refusal to withdraw fully before Hamas disarmed.

Iran, Hezbollah in Lebanon, armed groups in Syria and the Houthis in Yemen affect Israel’s security. Egypt and Jordan are peace-treaty partners. The Abraham Accords and possible further normalization concern regional diplomacy. The US relationship includes arms, diplomacy and disagreements over policy.

Keep asking: What is the political plan after the military operation?

Sources: AP · Withdrawal and disarmament dispute, August 2026

24

7 October: accountability after disaster

On 7 October 2023, Hamas-led attackers killed about 1,200 people, mostly civilians, and took 251 hostages. These events deeply affected Israeli families and public trust. The subsequent Gaza war brought immense civilian suffering to Palestinians.

Separate three levels of responsibility: intelligence assessment, military readiness and political decisions. An internal army inquiry can examine military conduct. A state commission of inquiry can examine a wider range of institutions and leaders, with legal powers.

The key political questions are who chooses the investigators, what they can examine and whether findings lead to change. Hostage negotiations were central during the war. AP reported recovery of the last Israeli hostage’s remains in January 2026; this guide therefore does not treat all hostages as still captive.

Keep asking: Would this inquiry be independent enough to examine the people who set it up?

PART VI

Israeli society

25

Identity: several stories in one country

Jewish Israelis may describe themselves as secular, traditional, religious or Haredi. These groups overlap with family origins, language and class. Ashkenazi usually refers to family origins in central and eastern Europe. Mizrahi refers broadly to origins in Middle Eastern and North African Jewish communities. Sephardi has related but distinct historical and religious meanings.

Russian-speaking and Ethiopian Israelis, French-speaking immigrants, English-speaking communities and others add different experiences. Olim are immigrants who came through aliyah. Some families have lived in the land for generations.

Arab citizens include Muslims, Christians and others. Druze identity is distinct and varied. Population trends matter for education, housing and budgets, but no community votes or thinks as one block.

Keep asking: Are you hearing people’s views, or assuming them from a label?

26

Arab citizens: citizenship, belonging and daily priorities

Arab citizens can vote in national elections and serve in the Knesset. Many identify as Palestinian citizens of Israel; others use different terms. National identity does not by itself tell you whether someone supports a particular party.

Arab communities face gaps in infrastructure, employment and access to land and planning. Organized crime and weak personal safety are major concerns. Schools, local authority budgets, policing and economic opportunity are linked.

Political choices include Arab-led parties, Jewish-led parties or not voting. Ra’am’s participation in the 2021 coalition showed one approach to gaining influence. Other parties prioritize opposition to occupation and collective rights. Arab citizens should not be confused with West Bank residents without Israeli citizenship.

Keep asking: Does a crime policy include prevention and public trust as well as enforcement?

Sources: IDI · Arab society: selected findings · IDI · Crime in Arab society (opinion and policy analysis)

27

Haredi society: faith, institutions and change

Haredim are Jews who follow a strict religious way of life. Communities differ: Hasidic, non-Hasidic Ashkenazi and Sephardi traditions have different institutions and leaders. Yeshivot are centers of religious study.

Education, family life and rabbinic leadership are important. Many women work; men’s employment patterns are strongly affected by religious study. Poverty and the cost of large households are major issues. Some Haredim serve in the army or civilian service; many oppose compulsory service.

Shas and United Torah Judaism seek influence over education, welfare and religious policy. Population growth makes their relationship with the wider economy and the army a long-term national question. Change inside Haredi society is real, but it is uneven.

Keep asking: Can integration policies protect religious freedom while sharing public duties?

Sources: IDI · Haredi society, 2025

28

Religious, traditional and secular Israelis

Religious Zionists connect Jewish religious life with building and defending the state. Some are closely tied to the settlement movement; others have different political views. “Religious Zionism” can mean a broad community or a specific political party—do not confuse them.

Masorti usually means traditional: a person may keep customs without following every religious rule. In Israeli social conversation, it does not necessarily mean membership in the Conservative/Masorti religious movement. Hiloni means secular.

Shabbat, public transport and school choices bring these identities into politics. Tel Aviv and Jerusalem are often used as symbols of secular and religious Israel, but both cities contain diverse communities.

Keep asking: Is the speaker describing a social group or a party?

29

Druze, Bedouin and other communities

The Druze have a distinct religion and close-knit communities. Many Druze men serve in the IDF. Service has not ended disagreements about equal citizenship, land and the Nation-State Basic Law.

Bedouin communities are Arab communities with different histories in the Negev and the north. Some live in recognized towns; others live in villages whose official status and services are disputed. Housing, planning, land claims and infrastructure are major political concerns.

Circassians, Armenians and several Christian communities also form part of society. Religion, language, legal status and national identity do not always match neatly. A minority group is not a single political voice.

Keep asking: What does equal access to services mean in this community?

30

Kibbutzim, moshavim and rural life

A kibbutz began as a collective community with shared work and resources. Many have since privatized parts of their economy. They can now combine agriculture, industry, tourism and ordinary salaried work.

A moshav is usually a village of separate households with cooperative arrangements. Regional councils provide services to groups of rural communities. Agriculture raises questions about water, workers, imports and food security.

Border communities became central to the debate after 7 October and fighting in the north. Rebuilding homes is only part of the task. Families also need schools, livelihoods, public services and confidence that they can return safely.

Keep asking: Does a reconstruction plan help people return, or only repair buildings?

PART VII

Religion and the state

31

Judaism and state institutions

The Chief Rabbinate has official roles in Jewish religious services, including marriage and aspects of conversion and kashrut. Religious councils provide local services. Religious courts deal with parts of personal status.

Kashrut means Jewish dietary law; certification concerns whether food follows it. Conversion concerns entry into Judaism. Recognition for immigration, religious marriage and community membership can involve different rules.

Disputes over the Western Wall and other holy sites concern access, religious practice and authority. Israel also has institutions for Muslim, Christian and Druze religious affairs. The political question is how much authority public bodies should give to religious institutions.

Keep asking: Is this rule about private belief, or a public service everyone must use?

32

Marriage, divorce and family life

Israel has no general domestic civil-marriage system open to everyone. Religious authorities conduct marriages within recognized communities. This can create barriers for interfaith couples, same-sex couples and people whose religious status is disputed.

There are important exceptions and alternatives. A limited civil-union register is available to qualifying couples recorded as having no religion. Israel registers many marriages performed abroad; registration is not the same question as every legal consequence of marriage.

Religious courts and family courts have different roles. Divorce can raise separate problems from marriage registration. Common-law partners and LGBTQ families have gained rights through legislation and courts. The election debate concerns expanding civil choice and reducing religious control.

Keep asking: Should citizens be able to choose a civil route inside Israel?

Sources: Ministry of Justice · Civil union for people without religion (Hebrew)

33

Religion in public life

Shabbat runs from Friday sunset to Saturday night. Many national transport services stop, but local initiatives and exceptions exist. Shops and restaurants vary by city, neighborhood and licensing rules.

The historical “status quo” describes arrangements on religion made around the state’s founding. It is not one clear, unchanging law. Marriage, education, food rules and public transport have all produced new disputes.

Supporters of religious rules stress Jewish identity and the ability to live observantly. Opponents stress personal freedom, equality and access to services. Gender separation raises especially difficult questions when it affects shared public space.

Keep asking: Could local choice solve the problem, and who would still be excluded?

PART VIII

Everyday life and services

34

Immigration, citizenship and residency

The Law of Return provides an immigration route for Jews and certain relatives, including children, grandchildren and qualifying spouses, subject to legal conditions. Being eligible for aliyah is not identical to being recognized as Jewish for religious marriage.

Citizenship and residency are different. Many Palestinians in East Jerusalem hold permanent residency rather than Israeli citizenship. Permanent residency alone does not give a Knesset vote. Citizenship, family reunification and temporary permits use different procedures.

Teudat zehut means identity card. Ulpan is a Hebrew course. Sal klita is an absorption-support package for eligible new immigrants. Population and Immigration Authority handles legal status; the Ministry of Aliyah and Integration handles many integration services.

Keep asking: Does a proposal change immigration eligibility, religious recognition, or both?

Sources: Knesset · Law of Return and family eligibility

35

Healthcare: the big picture

National health insurance covers Israeli residents under the law. People enroll in one of four health funds—kupot holim: Clalit, Maccabi, Meuhedet or Leumit. A health fund organizes doctors, referrals and many covered services.

Public coverage, supplementary health-fund insurance and private insurance are different layers. Having national coverage does not mean every treatment has no charge or no waiting time.

For politics, watch waiting times, staffing, mental healthcare, hospitals outside the center and what the health basket includes. A promise of “better healthcare” should say which service improves and how it will be funded. Emergency care is a separate issue from ordinary referrals; follow current official instructions when urgent care is needed.

Keep asking: Will this policy improve access for people who cannot pay privately?

Sources: Ministry of Aliyah · Health funds

36

Education: several school systems

Israel has state secular schools, state religious schools, Arab-language education and Haredi education, with further differences inside each group. Bagrut is the school-leaving examination certificate used for many study and work routes.

Universities, colleges, the Open University and vocational training offer different paths. The psychometric exam is one admissions tool; it is not the whole education system.

Core curriculum means subjects such as mathematics, English and science. The dispute is whether public funding should require these subjects in all schools. Teacher quality, special education, school buildings and gaps between communities also matter. A national average can hide very different opportunities for children.

Keep asking: What must every publicly funded school teach?

Sources: OECD · Economic Survey of Israel, 2025 · IDI · Haredi society, 2025

37

Work and employment

Israeli work includes high-tech, manufacturing, services, construction, agriculture and public employment. High-tech is economically important, but it is not everyone’s workplace.

A payslip can include tax, National Insurance, health contributions and pension deductions. Employment rights concern pay, leave, working time, parental rights and dismissal. Specific entitlements depend on current law and the job arrangement.

The Histadrut is a major labor federation. Unions bargain over pay and conditions and can call strikes. Employers, unions and government disagree about flexibility, protection and public-sector costs. Informal workplace culture does not remove formal obligations.

Keep asking: Does the plan raise productivity and pay, or only shift costs between workers and employers?

Sources: OECD · Economic Survey of Israel, 2025

38

Taxes, welfare and Bituach Leumi

Income tax is paid on income, with rates and credits defined by law. VAT is a tax on consumption. National Insurance contributions fund parts of social protection; health contributions help finance healthcare. These are different items.

Bituach Leumi is the National Insurance Institute. It administers benefits such as unemployment, disability and child allowances, subject to eligibility rules. Municipal welfare services also support families.

The political debate asks who contributes, who gets support and what the system should encourage. A benefit is not automatically available to every citizen. Compare who actually qualifies, rather than the headline amount.

Keep asking: Who pays for this benefit—and who is left outside it?

Sources: National Insurance · Benefits · National Insurance · What it does

39

Pensions and long-term savings

A pension fund saves money for retirement and may include insurance protections. Workplace arrangements generally involve employee and employer contributions. The state old-age benefit is separate from a workplace pension.

Kupat gemel is a provident savings product. Keren hishtalmut is a savings fund often offered through employment, with tax advantages under specified conditions. It is not available on identical terms to every worker.

For politics, ask whether people can build enough retirement income, whether fees reduce savings and how informal or low-paid work affects future security. For personal decisions, current contribution rules and your fund documents matter more than a general guide.

Keep asking: Does retirement policy protect people with interrupted or low-paid careers?

40

Banking and household finance

Banks provide accounts, payments and credit. Credit cards, direct debits, transfers and checks are different ways to pay. A mortgage is a loan secured against a home. Interest is the cost of borrowing money.

Inflation means a broad rise in prices over time. Lower inflation means prices rise more slowly; it does not necessarily mean prices fall. The Bank of Israel uses monetary policy, including its interest rate, to pursue price stability.

Higher interest rates can make borrowing harder while helping restrain inflation. Governments influence spending, tax, competition and housing supply. Distinguish the central bank’s tools from the cabinet’s tools.

Keep asking: Is the promised change about prices, wages or borrowing costs?

Sources: Bank of Israel · Objectives and functions

41

Housing and land

Housing prices reflect supply, demand, financing and planning. Public land management gives government a major role, but land release alone does not produce finished homes. Infrastructure, permits and construction capacity also matter.

Arnona is local property tax. Va’ad bayit is the building committee, often collecting money for shared expenses. Urban renewal can replace or strengthen older buildings. Pinui-binui usually means demolition and rebuilding within an approved project.

Renters and owners have different interests. Some policies lower purchase costs; others protect tenants or provide public housing. Faster building can conflict with local infrastructure or environmental needs. Ask where homes will be built and for whom.

Keep asking: Does the plan create usable homes near jobs and transport, or only announce units?

42

Transport and infrastructure

Rav-Kav is a public-transport payment card; approved payment apps are another option. Buses, railways and light rail connect different parts of the country. Current routes, fares and operating hours need a current transport source.

Rail investment, bus frequency, congestion and access outside major cities are national political choices. Shabbat transport adds a religion-and-state question. Local services can differ from the national network.

A future metro is a major planning and financing project, not a service already available everywhere. Good transport can reduce household costs and connect people to work, universities and hospitals.

Keep asking: Does the proposal help daily journeys now, or mainly promise distant projects?

Sources: Ministry of Transport · Public transport guide

PART IX

The economy

43

How the economy works

Israel’s economy combines a globally connected technology sector with many lower-productivity local services. Defense industries, finance, manufacturing, agriculture, tourism and construction also matter.

Exports earn income from abroad. Government spending pays for services, defense and transfers. Productivity means how much value workers create in a given time. Raising productivity helps support higher wages, but gains do not reach everyone automatically.

The OECD’s 2025 survey highlighted infrastructure, education, labor participation and competition as important challenges. War adds defense costs, reserve-duty disruption and uncertainty. A strong high-tech headline does not tell you whether ordinary families feel financially secure.

Keep asking: Will growth improve incomes across society?

Sources: OECD · Economic Survey of Israel, 2025

44

Why the cost of living is political

Housing, food, childcare, transport and taxes shape household budgets. Prices can stay high when imports face barriers, a few firms dominate markets or new housing is slow to arrive.

Competition reforms try to make it easier for suppliers to compete. Welfare policies help households afford essentials. Tax cuts can increase disposable income but may reduce money for services. These tools solve different problems.

The OECD identifies trade barriers, bureaucracy and weak competitive pressure as contributors to high costs. Compare proposals by asking how prices would fall, when the change would happen and who benefits. “Lower prices” without an explanation is a slogan.

Keep asking: What is the mechanism that makes the promised price cut possible?

Sources: OECD · Cost of living and reforms

PART X

The main political debates

45

Security and Palestinian policy: compare the choices

Ask every party the same questions. Does it support a Palestinian state, reject one, or postpone the issue? Does it favor settlement expansion or limits? Would it annex territory? What role should the Palestinian Authority have?

On Gaza, ask who should govern, how Hamas would be prevented from rearming and how civilians would receive services. On regional diplomacy, ask how a deal with Saudi Arabia or other countries connects to the Palestinian issue.

Right-wing parties often emphasize sovereignty and military control. The Zionist left generally emphasizes a negotiated separation alongside security. Centrist parties vary and may avoid a clear final-status promise. Arab parties generally emphasize Palestinian statehood and an end to occupation. These are broad patterns, not identical platforms.

Keep asking: Can the party explain a sustainable end state, not only an immediate action?

46

Religion and state: compare public choices

Civil marriage, conversion, the Chief Rabbinate, school funding and Shabbat transport affect everyday freedom. Some parties want national religious rules. Others want more civil options or local decisions.

Shas and UTJ generally defend Orthodox institutions and Haredi autonomy. Secular parties usually seek greater civil choice. Religious Zionists may support a strong Jewish public identity while disagreeing among themselves about practical reforms.

Coalition deals often decide what happens. A party may support Shabbat transport but join a government whose partners block it. Ask which reform is a firm condition for cooperation and which is only a preference.

Keep asking: Would the party spend coalition bargaining power on this issue?

47

Haredi conscription: the burden and the law

The historical exemption for full-time Torah students expanded greatly over time. Its Hebrew label is Torato Omanuto—Torah study is his occupation. Supporters say religious study preserves Jewish life and fear the army would weaken their community.

Critics say citizens should share danger, time and economic costs. Reservists and their families carry heavy burdens. Practical proposals include suitable military units, civilian service, gradual targets and limits on public support for non-compliance.

The legal position has changed through court rulings and new legislation. IDI’s July 2026 review describes legislation blocking arrest proceedings against yeshiva students who failed to report, and an interim Supreme Court order suspending it. That is a contested, changing position—not a simple permanent exemption.

Keep asking: Are targets enforceable, and what happens if they are missed?

Compare promises carefully

Shas and UTJ prioritize protecting religious study. Lieberman and several opposition lists emphasize broader compulsory service. But “equal service” can mean military service, civilian service or a mix. Ask about age, numbers, sanctions, religious accommodations and whether Arab citizens are included.

Sources: IDI · Review of institutional changes, July 2026

48

Judicial change: who gets the final say?

Supporters of reform argue that elected leaders need room to govern and that judges and legal officials have too much power. Opponents argue that independent courts protect rights and are especially important when a coalition controls both cabinet and parliament.

The main topics are judicial appointments, review of laws, the “reasonableness” test for government decisions, an override clause and the powers of legal advisers. An override clause would let parliament overcome certain court rulings; its effect depends on the exact design.

The Knesset passed judicial-selection legislation in March 2025. Later changes and court challenges make legal status important. A possible compromise might define review powers, protect basic rights and require broad agreement for major constitutional changes. This is a policy option, not a description of a settled consensus.

Keep asking: Would the same rule seem fair if your least-favorite party controlled the government?

Sources: Knesset · Judicial selection legislation, March 2025 · IDI · Review of institutional changes, July 2026

49

Economy and welfare: what should the state do?

Free-market approaches emphasize competition, private investment and fewer barriers. Welfare approaches emphasize public services, income support and reducing inequality. Most parties combine both in practice.

The key trade-off is not just “high tax” or “low tax.” Ask which tax changes, which service changes and whether the plan increases borrowing. Defense, healthcare, education and pensions compete for public money.

Haredi education budgets and Arab economic-development programs are often coalition issues. Compare rules consistently: what social need does the spending meet, what outcomes are measured and who is accountable? Avoid treating an entire community as a budget category.

Keep asking: Is the spending plan affordable without hiding costs?

Sources: OECD · Economic Survey of Israel, 2025 · National Insurance · Benefits

50

Education policy: the country’s future workforce

Core subjects can open routes to better jobs. Communities also want schools that reflect their religion, language and identity. The difficult question is how to protect both opportunity and freedom.

Public funding can be tied to a curriculum, inspection and measurable results. But enforcement, teacher training and transition periods matter. Arab schools may need buildings, staff and resources; Haredi schools differ in the amount of core teaching they provide.

Universities and research also affect the economy. Ask about teacher recruitment, special education and children outside strong school districts. A reform that only changes exams may leave deeper gaps untouched.

Keep asking: What would improve for a child starting school next year?

Sources: OECD · Economic Survey of Israel, 2025 · IDI · Haredi society, 2025

PART XI

Media, language and culture

51

News and polls: a calmer way to stay informed

Use several sources. Kan is public broadcasting. Channels 12, 13 and 14, newspapers and digital outlets have different audiences, ownership and editorial choices. English-language options include The Times of Israel, The Jerusalem Post and Haaretz; international agencies such as AP and Reuters add another perspective.

Separate reporting from opinion and party advertising. Read the date, named sources and the original document. A headline may describe a proposal as if it already happened. A clip may omit what came before it.

For polls, check the fieldwork dates, pollster, sample, method and list names. The margin of error for vote share is not simply a margin of error in seats. Near the threshold, a small change can turn several seats into none. A leadership-preference poll is not a Knesset-seat poll.

Keep asking: Can you trace this claim to evidence rather than repetition?

A ten-minute news routine

Read one short national summary. Check one primary document on an issue you care about. Compare how a second outlet frames the same event. For polls, look at several surveys over time. Avoid combining different hypothetical party lists into one supposed result.

Sources: AP · Small parties and the threshold, September 2026

52

Political language you will hear

Yamin means right; smol means left; merkaz means center. Gush means a political bloc. Bibi is Netanyahu’s nickname. Bibist usually means a strong Netanyahu supporter; it can be used as an insult. Rak lo Bibi means “anyone but Bibi.”

Mamlachti suggests commitment to state institutions and public responsibility. Meshilut means governability or the ability to enforce policy. Parties use these words positively, but may mean different things.

“Judicial reform,” “overhaul” and “judicial revolution” carry political judgments. Ask what specific rule is being discussed. The glossary gives Hebrew spellings and simple examples.

Keep asking: What concrete change is hidden behind the slogan?

53

Civic culture and public memory

Israeli public life is often informal and direct. Military service gives many people shared experiences, but not everyone serves or belongs to the same networks. Social access can affect opportunity.

Protektzia means using connections. Friyer means a sucker—someone taken advantage of. Combina means an informal arrangement, sometimes with a suggestion of bending rules. These words describe attitudes, not what every Israeli does.

Holocaust Remembrance Day, Memorial Day and Independence Day carry strong emotions. Shabbat and holidays affect public schedules. People can share ceremonies while disagreeing sharply about policy and history. For newcomers, listening to how others describe these days helps explain political feelings.

PART XII

A newcomer’s civic guide

54

A newcomer’s rights and responsibilities

Israeli citizens aged 18 or over generally have the right to vote in Knesset elections, subject to electoral law. Permanent residency alone is different. Check your electoral registration and assigned polling station with the official service.

Tax, National Insurance and military obligations depend on status and circumstances. Employment, consumer and tenant rights exist, but an overview cannot decide an individual case. Keep documents and use the relevant official service for current requirements.

Demonstrations, speech and access to public information are important parts of civic life, with legal limits. For election preparation, find your polling station and read the current instructions on identification, access and special voting arrangements.

Keep asking: Do you know which rights depend on citizenship and which on residency?

A simple election checklist

  1. Choose your three most important issues.
  2. Compare parties on those same issues.
  3. Check the leader’s record and the full candidate list.
  4. Check threshold risk using several recent polls.
  5. Ask which coalition your vote would support.
  6. Look for a funded plan, a deadline and an accountable institution.
  7. Before election day, check the official voting instructions and your polling station.

The guide does not recommend a party. Its aim is to help you understand the choices and ask better questions.
